We first examine the spatial distribution of compound climate events across sub-Saharan Africa at the level of 54,615 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) primary sampling units (PSUs) in 34 countries, which represent georeferenced population clusters, followed by their temporal evolution over four decades (1985–2024). We then investigate the directional asymmetry of extreme precipitation events (EPEs) around heatwaves (that is, the tendency for extreme precipitation to occur more frequently after heatwaves than before) and assess the role of drought in modulating heatwave characteristics. Finally, we combine the three event types into compound-hazard regimes. Heatwaves are defined relative to the local seasonal cycle (at least three consecutive days above the calendar-day 90th percentile of daily maximum temperature), EPEs as days above the local 95th percentile of wet-day precipitation, and drought as a 3-month Standardized Precipitation–Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI-3) below −1 (Methods).

## Spatial patterns of compound events

The three categories of compound climate events studied here exhibit distinct spatial patterns across sub-Saharan Africa (Fig. 1). Concurrent heatwave–extreme precipitation events are strongly influenced by background moisture availability, occurring predominantly in humid regions such as Madagascar and the Great Lakes region (Rwanda, Burundi, eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo), while remaining rare in arid zones including the Sahel and Namibia (Fig. 1a). Across the continent, these events occurred on average on 3.0 days per PSU during the study period. However, their distribution was highly heterogeneous. Nearly a quarter of all locations (24.0%) experienced no events, whereas a limited number of hotspots recorded disproportionately high frequencies, reaching up to 32 co-occurrence days per PSU. Moreover, heatwave days and extreme-precipitation days coincided far less often than expected if they occurred independently: the likelihood multiplication factor, which accounts for the shared seasonal cycle of the two hazards, was 0.32 (95% confidence interval (CI) 0.30–0.33), that is, about three times fewer co-occurrences than chance. It was lowest in Southern Africa (0.09, 95% CI 0.07–0.12) and highest in Central (0.42, 0.40–0.44) and Western Africa (0.41, 0.39–0.44) (Supplementary Table 7).

The temporal asymmetry of extreme precipitation around heatwaves is near-universal (Fig. 1b), with post-heatwave events exceeding pre-heatwave occurrences at 83.4% of PSUs. The ratio is highest in Southern Africa and in eastern Africa south of the equator, and lowest in the Congo basin, where some clusters show ratios below one.

Drought-preconditioned heatwaves follow a clear moisture gradient (Fig. 1c). Of the 9.6 million heatwaves detected, 29.7% of those with an available drought index were preceded by drought conditions (SPEI-3 < −1 in the month before onset). These events are most common in Southern Africa and the western Sahel, and least common in humid equatorial regions, consistent with local drought frequencies (Extended Data Fig. 7). Across latitudes, same-day co-occurrence peaks near the equator, whereas the asymmetry of extreme precipitation and drought preconditioning peak in the subtropics (Extended Data Fig. 4).

## Changes over four decades

Simultaneous heatwave–precipitation events have increased across the continent over the 1985–2024 period (Fig. 2a). At the continental scale, the compound event rate averaged 0.076 days per PSU per year and increased by 0.030 days per PSU per year per decade (95% CI 0.014–0.056). This intensification is spatially heterogeneous: the strongest increases occurred in Central (+0.054 per decade) and Eastern Africa (+0.037 per decade), particularly in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (+0.096 per decade), Burundi (+0.066), Madagascar (+0.066) and Rwanda (+0.064) (Supplementary Table 1). By contrast, Western (+0.016 per decade) and Southern Africa (+0.005, not significant) show the smallest changes in coincident events, reflecting the scarcity of extreme-precipitation days in arid environments and, in Southern Africa, the strong separation of heat and rainfall extremes. This increase largely follows the rise in heatwave frequency over the period: the share of heatwaves containing a co-occurrence day increased only modestly (+0.23 percentage points per decade).

Decadal maps reveal a progressive expansion of compound events (Fig. 3a), from isolated clusters in 1985–1994 to widespread high-density regions by 2015–2024, particularly across Madagascar, the East African coast and the Great Lakes region. This expansion indicates both an increasing frequency and a broadening spatial footprint of compound events across the continent.

In contrast, the after-to-before precipitation ratio (Fig. 2b) shows no significant trend over the study period, at the continental scale (−0.07 per decade, 95% CI −0.18 to 0.05) or in any sub-region. The absolute numbers of extreme-precipitation events both before and after heatwaves rose over the period, in line with the number of heatwaves (Extended Data Fig. 2), while their ratio remained above unity in every decade and region (Figs. 3b and 4c), confirming that the directional asymmetry persists through time.

Concurrently, the proportion of heatwaves preceded by drought increased continent-wide by 2.8 percentage points per decade (95% CI 1.2–4.4; Fig. 2d). The strongest increases occur in Southern Africa (+8.9 percentage points per decade), followed by Western Africa (+3.8) and Central Africa (+2.8), whereas the trend is not significant in Eastern Africa (+1.2). Decadal maps (Fig. 3c) show a progressive expansion of drought–heat coupling from localized regions in 1985–1994 to large parts of the Sahel and southern Africa by 2015–2024. This increase is not found with the precipitation-only 3-month Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI-3; −0.9 percentage points per decade, not significant; Extended Data Fig. 6), indicating that it is driven mainly by rising evaporative demand, which is estimated here from temperature, rather than by declining rainfall (see below).

## Extreme rainfall around heatwaves

Across sub-Saharan Africa, extreme precipitation occurs more often following heatwaves than preceding them (Fig. 4; Extended Data Fig. 1). Continent-wide, 2.37 million EPEs occurred within 30 days after a heatwave compared with 1.64 million before, yielding an aggregated after-to-before ratio of 1.44. This asymmetry is not a product of the rainy-season calendar alone: when each heatwave was moved to the same time of year in other years (1,000 permutations; Methods), the ratio was 1.01 (95% envelope 1.005–1.023), so that the observed ratio is 1.42 times the value expected from seasonality (Extended Data Fig. 1a,d). The excess was found in every sub-region (×1.21 in Western, ×1.60 in Eastern, ×1.20 in Central and ×2.21 in Southern Africa) and in every season of heatwave onset (Extended Data Fig. 1c; Supplementary Table 4). It arises mainly from a deficit of extreme precipitation before heatwaves: compared with the same season in other years, heatwaves were preceded by 37% fewer EPEs (0.63 of the null median), whereas EPEs after heatwaves were only 10% fewer (0.90) (Extended Data Fig. 1e). This deficit before heatwaves was strongest in Southern Africa (0.42).

This asymmetry is strongest in Southern Africa and south-eastern Africa (Namibia 2.61, Zimbabwe 2.60, Eswatini 2.23, South Africa 2.14), where rainfall is concentrated in a single summer season and heatwaves tend to occur at the end of the dry season. Conversely, the asymmetry is weaker in humid regions where background moisture supports EPEs both before and after heatwaves, such as the Gulf of Guinea coast and Cameroon (ratios 1.05–1.11). This pattern holds across 33 of the 34 countries analysed, with Guinea (ratio 0.93) as the sole exception (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Table 1).

The timing of these events is non-random: extreme precipitation is most likely on the first day after a heatwave ends and remains elevated during the following two weeks (Fig. 4b), a pattern that remains consistent across all four decades. The asymmetry follows the rainfall seasons (Extended Data Fig. 3): in Western Africa, the ratio reaches 8.3 for heatwaves starting in February and 6.3 in May, before the monsoon, but falls to 0.1–0.5 for heatwaves starting from September to December, after the rains; in Southern Africa it reaches 11 in September and 5.5 in October, before the rainy season.

Over time (Fig. 4c), the ratio decreases slightly after the first decade (continental 1.7 in 1985–1994 and 1.4–1.5 thereafter), Southern Africa maintains the highest ratios throughout (2.5 to 2.0), while Western and Central Africa maintain the lowest (1.4 to 1.2). In every region and every decade, the ratio remains above unity, confirming that this directional asymmetry is a persistent feature of the African climate system rather than a statistical artefact of any particular period. Sensitivity tests across temporal windows from ±7 to ±60 days yield the same qualitative pattern (ratio 1.73 to 1.33; Extended Data Fig. 5a,b), ruling out window size as a driver of the asymmetry. Rainfall can terminate a heatwave, and 7.9% of post-heatwave EPEs fall on the first day after its end; excluding EPEs within 1 to 5 days of the heatwave boundaries reduces the ratio only to 1.35–1.38 (Supplementary Table 5), attesting to the robustness of the results.

## Drought before heatwaves

Heatwaves that occur after drought conditions last longer across all regions (Fig. 5a; Extended Data Fig. 5d). On average, these events are extended by 0.3 days (4.88 versus 4.57 days, +7%), with the most pronounced prolongations occurring in Southern Africa (+0.9 days, +20%) and the smallest in Western Africa (+0.1 days, +3%), and the extension increases with drought severity (+5% for SPEI-3 < −0.5 to +12% for SPEI-3 < −2). The effect on temperature is more variable (Fig. 5b): on average, heatwaves after drought are 1.2 °C hotter (peak daily maximum temperature 33.6 versus 32.4 °C); in Southern Africa, they are substantially hotter (+2.7 °C), whereas other regions exhibit more modest increases (+1.5 °C in Western, +0.9 °C in Eastern and +0.3 °C in Central Africa). Because SPEI depends on temperature through evapotranspiration, part of this difference may reflect the persistence of warm conditions from the preceding month.

The proportion of heatwaves preceded by drought varied between decades, from 29.1% in 1985–1994 and 23.3% in 1995–2004 to 28.1% in 2005–2014 and 32.4% in 2015–2024 (Fig. 5c). Southern Africa shows the steepest pathway, with drought-preconditioned heatwaves reaching 57.5% in the most recent decade. Part of these values reflects where and when heatwaves occur and the background increase in drought frequency, which is itself higher than nominal after 2014 (over 1985–2024, 19.5% of all months had SPEI-3 < −1, compared with about 16% for a standardized index; Extended Data Fig. 7).

Compared with the drought frequency expected for the same cluster, calendar month and decade (23.5%), heatwaves were preceded by drought 1.26 times more often (Extended Data Fig. 8; Supplementary Table 6). This heatwave-specific excess was also found with the precipitation-only SPI-3 (19.3% observed versus 15.9% expected, ×1.21). It reflects a genuine rainfall deficit before heatwaves in Southern (SPI-3 ×1.42) and Eastern Africa (×1.33), whereas in Western and Central Africa it mainly reflects evaporative demand (SPEI-3 ×1.18, SPI-3 ×1.07–1.11). Using the onset month rather than the preceding month raised the share of drought-preconditioned heatwaves to about 38% (Extended Data Figs. 5e and 6), consistent with the heatwave itself increasing evaporative demand in its own month.

## Compound-hazard regimes

Classification of PSUs by their dominant compound event combinations reveals that most locations across sub-Saharan Africa are exposed to at most one prominent hazard type (Fig. 6). Using the upper quartile of each metric as threshold (Methods), 44.0% of the 53,370 classifiable locations had no prominent event type (Fig. 6a). Single-hazard regimes were the most common otherwise: heat–precipitation events alone (15.2%), post-heatwave precipitation excess alone (14.6%) and drought–heat events alone (13.9%). The combination of drought–heat coupling and post-heatwave precipitation excess represents 9.0% of locations, while the pairing of simultaneous heat–precipitation events with drought–heat coupling (1.8%) or with post-heatwave precipitation excess (1.2%), and the overlap of all three types (0.2%), constitute the least frequent categories.

These shares reflect how the event types relate to each other. Drought–heat coupling and post-heatwave precipitation excess were prominent together 1.47 times more often than expected if they were independent, consistent with heatwaves occurring at the end of the dry season, just before the rains. Conversely, simultaneous heat–precipitation events were rarely prominent together with post-heatwave precipitation excess (0.29 times the expected frequency) or with drought–heat coupling (0.43 times).

These regimes exhibit strong geographical variation (Fig. 6b): heat–precipitation regimes dominate in humid regions (Madagascar, the Gulf of Guinea coast, the Great Lakes), whereas drought–heat regimes and their combination with post-heatwave precipitation excess dominate in Southern Africa and the western Sahel, reflecting dry baseline conditions. **[Check against Fig. 6b: description of Eastern Africa.]** Over time, regimes dominated by heat–precipitation co-occurrence expanded (from 3.6% of classifiable locations in 1985–1994 to 30.0% in 2015–2024), whereas post-heatwave precipitation excess alone became less frequent (26.0% to 7.7%) (Fig. 6c–e). The same shifts were found among the 18,564 locations classifiable in both decades; at the level of individual locations, however, the regime was unchanged in only 18.3% of cases (14.5% expected by chance), so these changes describe a net redistribution rather than stable local trajectories (Fig. 6f). The same patterns were found when the upper tercile was used as threshold (Extended Data Fig. 9).

## Sensitivity to the heatwave threshold

The main results were reproduced with a single annual heatwave threshold instead of a calendar-day threshold (Extended Data Fig. 10; Supplementary Figs. 1–3). In that case heatwaves concentrate in the hot season, which raises the after-to-before ratio (3.11) but also its seasonal expectation (1.84); the observed ratio still exceeds this expectation (×1.69), and the deficit of extreme precipitation before heatwaves remains nearly identical (0.63 of the null median).
