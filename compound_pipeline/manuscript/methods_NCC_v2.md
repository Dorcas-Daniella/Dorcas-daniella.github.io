# Methods

*Nature Climate Change format: Methods placed after the main-text references, short bold subheadings, references numbered in continuation of the main text (here written as [ref. Author year] for conversion). Items in [square brackets] must be completed by the authors. All counts and parameters refer to the final run (`run_manifest.json`, run status "completed").*

---

**Study population.** We used the GPS coordinates of the primary sampling units (PSUs; survey clusters) of the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) conducted in 34 sub-Saharan African countries [list of surveys and years: Supplementary Table 2]. Records with missing coordinates, coordinates at (0°, 0°) or outside 35° S–25° N and 18° W–52° E were removed, and PSUs sharing exactly the same coordinates across survey rounds were retained once (earliest survey). The final sample comprises 54,615 PSUs. Countries were grouped into the four United Nations M49 sub-regions: Western, Eastern, Middle (hereafter Central) and Southern Africa (Supplementary Table 1). DHS coordinates are randomly displaced by up to 2 km in urban and 5 km in rural clusters (10 km for 1% of rural clusters) [ref. DHS GPS documentation]; this displacement is small relative to the ERA5 grid (0.25°) but may shift the matched CHIRPS cell (0.05°). Each PSU is treated as a sampled locality and no demographic weighting is applied.

**Climate data.** Daily maximum and minimum 2-m air temperature (Tmax, Tmin) were taken from the ERA5 reanalysis at 0.25° resolution [ref. Hersbach et al. 2020], and daily precipitation from CHIRPS v2.0 at 0.05° resolution [ref. Funk et al. 2015], for 1 January 1985 to 31 December 2024. Each PSU was assigned the nearest grid cell of each product; a match was rejected when the cell centre lay more than 20 km away (about the half-diagonal of a 0.25° cell at the equator) or when the cell contained no valid data. ERA5 daily values follow UTC days, whereas CHIRPS days follow the reporting convention of its gauge inputs; a possible one-day offset between the two products could not be quantified.

**Eligibility.** All 54,615 PSUs had a valid ERA5 cell. A valid CHIRPS cell was available for 53,802 PSUs (813 PSUs matched only cells without valid data). The extreme-precipitation threshold additionally required at least 100 wet days in the reference period, which excluded 20 further PSUs. Analyses involving precipitation extremes (Axes I and II, the likelihood multiplication factor and the null model) therefore use 53,782 PSUs, and analyses of drought (Axis III) use the 53,802 PSUs with both ERA5 and CHIRPS data. The same population enters numerators, denominators, tables and figures of each axis. The 54,615 PSUs fall into 10,713 distinct ERA5 cells (5.1 PSUs per cell on average). Because PSUs in the same ERA5 cell share identical temperature series, this pseudo-replication is accounted for in the uncertainty estimates and in the null model (see below).

**Reference period.** All percentile thresholds and the calibration of the drought indices use 1985–2014. Thresholds estimated in this period were applied to all years without in-base correction, so exceedance frequencies inside the reference period are slightly biased low relative to 2015–2024 [ref. Zhang et al. 2005].

**Heatwaves.** A heatwave (HW) is a run of at least three consecutive days with Tmax strictly above the 90th percentile threshold of the PSU. In the primary analysis (calendar-day threshold), the threshold is computed for each calendar day from the reference-period Tmax values within a 31-day window centred on that day [ref. Russo et al. 2014], on a fixed 366-position calendar in which 29 February occupies its own position and contributes data only in leap years. The threshold therefore follows the seasonal cycle, and heatwaves are anomalies relative to the time of year. As a sensitivity analysis (annual threshold), a single 90th percentile of all reference-period days was used for each PSU, so that heatwaves concentrate in the hot season. For each heatwave we recorded the onset and end dates, the duration and the peak Tmax. Over all PSUs, 9,608,094 heatwaves were detected with the calendar-day threshold (9,449,139 at the 53,782 PSUs eligible for Axes I and II) and 10,743,161 with the annual threshold.

**Extreme precipitation.** An extreme precipitation event (EPE) day is a day with precipitation strictly greater than the PSU-specific 95th percentile of wet-day (≥1 mm) precipitation in the reference period. In total, 12,787,839 EPE days were identified.

**Drought indices.** Meteorological drought was characterized with the 3-month Standardized Precipitation–Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI-3) [ref. Vicente-Serrano et al. 2010], computed from the monthly climatic water balance (precipitation minus potential evapotranspiration, PET). PET was estimated with the Hargreaves–Samani equation [ref. Hargreaves & Samani 1985] from Tmax, Tmin and extraterrestrial radiation [ref. Allen et al. 1998], the only formulation compatible with the available variables. A month was used only if at least 90% of its days had valid data. A log-logistic distribution was fitted by maximum likelihood for each calendar month on 1985–2014 with xclim 0.61.1 [ref. Bourgault et al. 2023]; values for 2015–2024 are therefore extrapolations outside the calibration period. The 3-month Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI-3) [ref. McKee et al. 1993], fitted with a gamma distribution and a separate probability for zero totals, was computed in the same way as a precipitation-only alternative that does not depend on temperature. Drought is defined as an index value below −1. The first index value is March 1985. Every missing value was assigned to a documented cause (ineligible PSU, spin-up months, missing input or insufficient calibration data), and an unexplained missing value stopped the computation.

**Axis I: same-day co-occurrence.** A co-occurrence day is a day that is both a heatwave day and an EPE day at the same PSU. We report the number of co-occurrence days per PSU per year, averaged over all eligible PSUs (zeros included), and the percentage of heatwaves containing at least one co-occurrence day. To test whether heat and extreme rainfall co-occur more or less often than expected from their individual frequencies, we computed the likelihood multiplication factor (LMF) [ref. Zscheischler & Seneviratne 2017]:

LMF = N_joint / E[N_joint],     (1)

where N_joint is the observed number of co-occurrence days. In the naive version, E[N_joint] = N_hot N_EPE / N_valid, with all days pooled. Because both extremes have marked seasonal cycles, the primary seasonal version conditions the expectation on the day of the year d:

E_seasonal[N_joint] = Σ_d N_hot(d) N_EPE(d) / N_valid(d).     (2)

All four counts are computed on the same days (both Tmax and precipitation available). LMF < 1 indicates that the two extremes co-occur less often than expected under independence. The LMF is pooled over PSUs (ratio of summed observed to summed expected counts), and its 95% confidence interval was obtained from 1,000 bootstrap resamples of ERA5 cells, so that PSUs sharing a temperature series are resampled together; a PSU-level bootstrap is reported for comparison. The day-of-year conditioning pools all years and therefore does not account for interannual non-stationarity.

**Axis II: extreme precipitation before and after heatwaves.** For each heatwave we considered a window of W = 30 days before onset and W = 30 days after the end. Heatwaves whose window extended beyond the study period were excluded (87,380 heatwaves at eligible PSUs with the calendar-day threshold; 67,625 with the annual threshold), leaving 9,361,759 heatwaves (10,501,242 with the annual threshold). Each EPE located within the window of at least one heatwave of the same PSU was attributed to a single heatwave, the nearest one (distance to onset for an EPE before it, to its end for an EPE after it; ties assigned to the earlier heatwave); EPEs falling during a heatwave belong to Axis I. The primary metric is the after/before count ratio,

R = N_after / N_before,     (3)

computed as a ratio of sums over all heatwaves of a group (continent, region, country, decade). R > 1 means that extreme precipitation is more frequent after heatwaves than before them. As secondary metrics, following event coincidence analysis [ref. Donges et al. 2016], we computed for each heatwave a precursor indicator (at least one EPE in the 30 days before onset) and a trigger indicator (at least one EPE in the 30 days after the end), and report their PSU-averaged difference (trigger − precursor). These indicators are evaluated for each heatwave separately, so an EPE may inform several heatwaves; the primary ratio avoids this by unique attribution.

**Seasonally constrained null model.** Because heat and rainfall both follow strong seasonal cycles, an after/before asymmetry can arise from the timing of heatwaves relative to the rainy season alone. We therefore compared R with a null distribution that preserves location, duration and time of year. In each of 1,000 permutations, every heatwave kept its PSU and its duration, and its onset date (month and day) was moved to another year and shifted by up to ±15 days (29 February mapped to 28 February in non-leap years). A displacement was admissible only if the full ±30-day window stayed within the study period, the new onset fell in a different calendar year from the observed one, and the window did not overlap the observed heatwave. The target year was drawn uniformly among the years with at least one admissible shift, then the shift uniformly among the admissible values. PSUs in the same ERA5 cell have identical heatwaves; one draw was therefore made per distinct heatwave (ERA5 cell, onset, end), giving 1,830,045 independent units with the calendar-day threshold (2,071,704 with the annual threshold), and copied to all PSUs of that cell. Surrogate heatwaves were processed with exactly the same attribution procedure as the observed ones, and all 1,000 permutations were usable for every region and season. The analysis is descriptive: we report the observed ratio, the median and 2.5–97.5 percentile envelope of the null, and the ratio of the observed value to the null median. The null also retains the numbers of EPEs before and after the surrogate heatwaves, which decomposes the asymmetry into a deficit of EPEs before heatwaves (observed / null median of N_before < 1) and/or an excess after them (observed / null median of N_after > 1). Results are given for the continent, each region and each season of heatwave onset (December–February, March–May, June–August, September–November). One-sided permutation p-values were computed but are not used for inference. Because the null moves heatwaves between years, it controls for location and seasonality but not for interannual variability; the decomposition therefore describes whether the periods surrounding observed heatwaves are drier or wetter than the same season in other years, without identifying the physical driver.

**Sensitivity of Axis II.** We repeated the calculation (i) with windows of W = 7, 15, 45 and 60 days, truncation being re-evaluated for each W, and (ii) after excluding EPEs within 1, 2, 3 or 5 days of the heatwave boundaries, to assess the contribution of rainfall that terminates a heatwave (the null was not recomputed for these variants). At W = 30 days, the observed numbers of heatwaves, EPEs before and after, and event-coincidence rates were verified to be identical in the main analysis, the null model and the window sensitivity, for the continent and each region.

**Axis III: heatwaves preceded by drought.** For each heatwave we extracted SPEI-3 for the calendar month preceding the onset month (lag 1). Using the preceding month avoids the circularity of the onset month, whose PET is inflated by the heatwave itself. The primary metric is the percentage of heatwaves preceded by drought (SPEI-3 < −1) among heatwaves for which the index is available (99.7% of heatwaves at the 53,802 eligible PSUs with the calendar-day threshold; heatwaves with onset in January–March 1985 have no antecedent value). Sensitivity analyses used SPEI-3 in the onset month (lag 0), SPI-3 at lag 1 and drought thresholds of −0.5, −1.5 and −2. We also compared the duration and peak Tmax of heatwaves with and without antecedent drought.

Because drought frequency differs between places and seasons and, for SPEI, increases after the end of the calibration period, the observed percentage was compared with its local expectation. For each heatwave, the expected probability of drought is the frequency of drought at the same PSU in the same calendar month, either over all years (location and season) or within the same decade (location, season and background trend):

ratio_local = (share of heatwaves preceded by drought) / (mean expected probability over the same heatwaves).     (4)

A ratio above 1 indicates that heatwaves are preceded by drought more often than local conditions alone imply. It was computed for SPEI-3 and SPI-3, for the continent and each region; comparing the two indices separates a precipitation deficit (captured by both) from an increase in evaporative demand (captured by SPEI-3 only).

**Aggregation.** Metrics were first computed per PSU and then aggregated by country, region, the continent, year and decade (1985–1994, 1995–2004, 2005–2014 and 2015–2024). Levels are reported as event-pooled values (ratio of sums for R; share of all heatwaves for percentages) unless stated otherwise. Temporal trends were computed on annual series: the yearly ratio of sums for R, the yearly mean over PSUs (zeros included) for co-occurrence days, and the yearly mean of per-PSU values for the event-coincidence difference and the drought percentages. Heatwaves and Axis II quantities are dated by the onset year of the heatwave, and co-occurrence days by their own date.

**Temporal trends.** Linear trends of the annual series (1985–2024) were estimated with the Theil–Sen slope [ref. Sen 1968] and expressed per decade. Monotonic trends were tested with the Mann–Kendall test using a conservative Hamed–Rao correction for autocorrelation [ref. Hamed & Rao 1998], in which only significant autocorrelation lags enter the correction and the correction is applied only when it increases the variance. The 95% confidence interval of each slope was obtained from 1,000 moving-block bootstrap resamples with 5-year blocks [ref. Künsch 1989]. Country-level tests were corrected for multiple testing with the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (α = 0.05) [ref. Benjamini & Hochberg 1995], separately for each axis and metric. Series with an internal gap keep their slope, but no test or interval is computed. A trend is described as significant when the Mann–Kendall test (P < 0.05) and the bootstrap interval agree, and as weak when they disagree.

**Compound-hazard regimes.** To summarize the three axes jointly, each PSU was classified according to which axes are prominent at that location. The three metrics are co-occurrence days per year (Axis I), the after/before ratio (Axis II; set to +∞ when no EPE precedes but at least one follows the heatwaves) and the percentage of heatwaves preceded by drought (Axis III). An axis is prominent when its value is strictly above the 75th percentile (order statistic x₍⌈0.75n⌉₎) of the PSUs classifiable on all three axes over 1985–2024, which yields eight mutually exclusive regimes from "none prominent" to "all three"; the upper tercile was used as a sensitivity threshold. A PSU was classifiable only if at least 10 EPEs were attributed to its heatwaves (Axis II) and at least 10 of its heatwaves had an antecedent SPEI-3 value (Axis III). These minimum supports were fixed from the distribution of supports alone, before thresholds or classes were examined; over 1985–2024 they retain 99.2% (Axis II) and 100% (Axis III) of eligible PSUs with the calendar-day threshold, and 53,370 PSUs are classifiable on all three axes. PSUs that are ineligible or below the minimum supports are labelled "insufficient data". The co-prominence of two axes was compared with the product of their individual prominence frequencies (expectation under independence). Decadal maps apply the same 1985–2024 thresholds to each decade, so their changes combine changes in hazard frequency and, for Axis II, the larger sampling variability of decadal ratios (median of 8 attributed EPEs per PSU in 1985–1994). Transitions between 1985–1994 and 2015–2024 were computed on the 18,564 PSUs classifiable in both decades, and the proportion of PSUs keeping the same regime was compared with the proportion expected by chance given the regime frequencies of each decade.

**Threshold sensitivity.** The full analysis (all axes, null model, trends and regimes) was repeated with the annual heatwave threshold. The calendar-day threshold is used as the primary analysis because it defines heatwaves as anomalies relative to the time of year, which makes the three axes less dependent on the seasonal cycle; results with the annual threshold are reported in the Supplementary Information.

**Statistics and reproducibility.** All intervals are 95% intervals: cluster-bootstrap confidence intervals for the LMF, 2.5–97.5 percentile envelopes for the null model and moving-block bootstrap confidence intervals for trends. Sample sizes are the numbers of PSUs, heatwaves and EPEs reported above. All computations were performed in Python 3.11.7 with NumPy 1.26.4, pandas 2.3.3, xarray 2025.9.1, SciPy 1.16.2, xclim 0.61.1 and statsmodels 0.14.0, and maps were drawn with Cartopy 0.25.0. Random draws used a fixed seed (42). A run manifest records the configuration, package versions, the identity of each input file, a fingerprint of the code, the eligibility exclusions, the effective fitting methods of SPEI and SPI, the number of usable permutations and the outcome of internal consistency checks; it is marked as completed only at the end of the run.
