## Results

We first examine the spatial distribution of compound climate events across sub-Saharan Africa at the level of 54,615 Demographic and Health Survey primary sampling units (PSUs), which represent georeferenced population clusters, followed by their temporal evolution over four decades. We then investigate the directional asymmetry of extreme precipitation around heatwaves (that is, the tendency for extreme precipitation to occur more frequently after heatwaves than before) and assess the role of drought in modulating heatwave characteristics.

The three categories of compound climate events studied here exhibit distinct spatial patterns across sub-Saharan Africa (Fig. 1). Concurrent heatwave–extreme precipitation events are strongly influenced by background moisture availability, occurring predominantly in humid regions such as Madagascar and the Great Lakes region (Rwanda, Burundi, eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo), while remaining rare in arid zones including the Sahel and Namibia (Fig. 1a). Across the continent, these events occurred on average 3.0 times per PSU during the study period (0.076 days per PSU per year). However, their distribution was highly heterogeneous. Nearly a quarter of all locations (24.0%) experienced no events, whereas a limited number of hotspots recorded disproportionately high frequencies, reaching up to 32 events per PSU. Moreover, heatwave days and extreme-precipitation days coincided far less often than expected if they occurred independently: the likelihood multiplication factor, which accounts for the shared seasonal cycle of the two hazards, was 0.32 (95% CI 0.30–0.33), that is, about three times fewer co-occurrences than chance. It was lowest in Southern Africa (0.09 [0.07, 0.12]) and highest in Central (0.42 [0.40, 0.44]) and Western Africa (0.41 [0.39, 0.44]) (Extended Data Table S7).

**Fig. 1 | Spatial distribution of compound climate events across sub-Saharan Africa (1985–2024).** **a**, Frequency of same-day heatwave–extreme-precipitation co-occurrence (co-occurrence days per cluster per year). **b**, Ratio of extreme-precipitation events occurring after versus before heatwaves (grey: no extreme precipitation before heatwaves, ratio undefined). **c**, Percentage of heatwaves preconditioned by drought (SPEI-3 < −1 in the month preceding onset). Each point is one of the 54,615 Demographic and Health Survey clusters across 34 countries; metrics derived from ERA5 temperature, CHIRPS precipitation and SPEI-3.

The temporal asymmetry of extreme precipitation around heatwaves is near-universal (Fig. 1b), with post-heatwave events exceeding pre-heatwave occurrences at 83.4% of PSUs. The ratio is highest in Southern Africa and in eastern Africa south of the equator, and lowest in the Congo basin, where some clusters show ratios below one.

Drought-preconditioned heatwaves follow a clear moisture gradient (Fig. 1c). Of the 9.6 million heatwaves detected, 29.7% of those with an available drought index were preceded by drought conditions (SPEI-3 < −1 in the month before onset). These events are most common in Southern Africa and the western Sahel, and least common in humid equatorial regions, consistent with local drought frequencies (Extended Data Fig. S7). Across latitudes, same-day co-occurrence peaks near the equator, whereas the asymmetry of extreme precipitation and drought preconditioning peak in the subtropics (Extended Data Fig. S4).

Simultaneous heatwave–precipitation events have increased across the continent over the 1985–2024 period (Fig. 2a). At the continental scale, the compound event rate averaged 0.076 events per PSU per year and increased by +0.030 events per PSU per decade (95% CI 0.014–0.056). This intensification is spatially heterogeneous: the strongest increases occurred in Central (+0.054 per decade) and Eastern Africa (+0.037 per decade), particularly in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (+0.096 events per decade), Burundi (+0.066), Madagascar (+0.066) and Rwanda (+0.064) (Extended Data Table S1). By contrast, Western (+0.016 per decade) and Southern Africa (+0.005, not significant) show the smallest changes in coincident events despite high heatwave frequency, reflecting the low probability of extreme precipitation co-occurring with heat extremes in arid environments. This increase largely follows the rise in heatwave frequency over the period: the share of heatwaves containing a co-occurrence day increased only modestly (+0.23 percentage points per decade).

**Fig. 2 | Temporal trends in compound climate events (1985–2024).** Columns, sub-Saharan Africa as a whole and the four UN sub-regions (Western, Eastern, Central, Southern); rows, **a**, same-day heat–precipitation co-occurrence (days per cluster per year); **b**, after-versus-before precipitation ratio around heatwaves (annual ratio of totals); **c**, difference between the shares of heatwaves followed and preceded by extreme precipitation (secondary measure of asymmetry); **d**, proportion of heatwaves preceded by drought. Points, annual values; smoothed curves; trend lines and per-decade slopes estimated by Theil–Sen regression (95% block-bootstrap CI; * Mann–Kendall p < 0.05).

Decadal maps reveal a progressive expansion of compound events (Fig. 3a), from isolated clusters in 1985–1994 to widespread high-density regions by 2015–2024, particularly across Madagascar, the East African coast and the Great Lakes region. This expansion indicates both an increasing frequency and a broadening spatial footprint of compound events across the continent.

In contrast, the after-to-before precipitation ratio (Fig. 2b) shows no significant trend over the study period, at the continental scale (−0.07 per decade, 95% CI −0.18 to 0.05) or in any sub-region. The absolute numbers of extreme-precipitation events both before and after heatwaves rose over the period, in line with the number of heatwaves (Extended Data Fig. S2), while their ratio remained above unity in every decade and region (Fig. 3b; Fig. 4c), confirming that the directional asymmetry persists through time. Concurrently, the proportion of heatwaves preceded by drought increased continent-wide by +2.8 percentage points per decade (95% CI 1.2–4.4; Fig. 2d). The strongest increases occur in Southern Africa (+8.9 percentage points per decade), followed by Western Africa (+3.8) and Central Africa (+2.8), whereas the trend is not significant in Eastern Africa (+1.2). Decadal maps (Fig. 3c) show a progressive expansion of drought-heat coupling from localized regions in 1985–1994 to large parts of the Sahel and southern Africa by 2015–2024. This increase is not found with the precipitation-only SPI-3 (−0.9 percentage points per decade, not significant) (Extended Data Fig. S6), indicating that it is driven mainly by rising evaporative demand rather than by declining rainfall (see below).

**Fig. 3 | Spatio-temporal evolution of compound events by decade across sub-Saharan Africa, 1985–2024.** Cluster-level values for the three event types (**a**, concurrent heatwave–extreme precipitation events; **b**, after-to-before EPE ratio for sequential events, grey where undefined; **c**, proportion of drought-preconditioned heatwaves) aggregated over four decades (1985–1994, 1995–2004, 2005–2014, 2015–2024), showing the spatial redistribution of each metric through time.

Across sub-Saharan Africa, extreme precipitation occurs more often following heatwaves than preceding them (Fig. 4; Extended Data Fig. S1). Continent-wide, 2.37 million EPEs occurred within 30 days after a heatwave compared with 1.64 million before, yielding an aggregated after-to-before ratio of 1.44. This asymmetry is not a product of the rainy-season calendar alone: when each heatwave was moved to the same time of year in other years, the ratio was 1.01 (95% envelope 1.005–1.023), so that the observed ratio is 1.42 times the value expected from seasonality (Extended Data Fig. S1a,d). The excess was found in every sub-region (×1.21 in Western, ×1.60 in Eastern, ×1.20 in Central and ×2.21 in Southern Africa) and in every season of heatwave onset (Extended Data Fig. S1c). It arises mainly from a deficit of extreme precipitation before heatwaves: compared with the same season in other years, heatwaves were preceded by 37% fewer EPEs (0.63 of the null median), whereas EPEs after heatwaves were only 10% fewer (0.90) (Extended Data Fig. S1e). This deficit before heatwaves was strongest in Southern Africa (0.42).

This asymmetry is strongest in Southern Africa and south-eastern Africa (Namibia 2.61, Zimbabwe 2.60, Eswatini 2.23, South Africa 2.14), where rainfall is concentrated in a single summer season and heatwaves tend to occur at the end of the dry season. Conversely, the asymmetry is weaker in humid regions where background moisture supports EPE both before and after heatwaves, such as the Gulf of Guinea coast and Cameroon (ratios 1.05–1.11). This pattern holds across 33 of the 34 countries analyzed, with Guinea (ratio 0.93) as the sole exception (Fig. 4a; Extended Data Table S1).

The timing of these events is non-random: extreme precipitation is most likely on the first day after a heatwave ends and remains elevated during the following two weeks (Fig. 4b), a pattern that remains consistent across all four decades. The asymmetry follows the rainfall seasons (Extended Data Fig. S3): in Western Africa, the ratio reaches 8.3 for heatwaves starting in February and 6.3 in May, before the monsoon, but falls to 0.1–0.5 for heatwaves starting from September to December, after the rains; in Southern Africa it reaches 11 in September and 5.5 in October, before the rainy season. Over time (Fig. 4c), the ratio decreases slightly after the first decade (continental 1.7 in 1985–1994 and 1.4–1.5 thereafter), Southern Africa maintains the highest ratios throughout (2.5 to 2.0), while Western and Central Africa maintain the lowest (1.4 to 1.2). In every region and every decade, the ratio remains above unity, confirming that this directional asymmetry is a persistent feature of the African climate system rather than a statistical artifact of any particular period. Sensitivity tests across temporal windows from ±7 to ±60 days yield the same qualitative pattern (ratio 1.73 to 1.33; Extended Data Fig. S5a,b), ruling out window size as a driver of the asymmetry. Because rainfall can terminate a heatwave, 7.9% of post-heatwave EPEs fall on the day after it ends; excluding EPEs within 1 to 5 days of the heatwave boundaries reduces the ratio only to 1.35–1.38 (Extended Data Table S5), attesting to the robustness of the results.

**Fig. 4 | EPE temporal asymmetry around heatwaves.** **a**, Country-mean counts of extreme-precipitation events per cluster before versus after heatwaves; the 1:1 line denotes temporal symmetry. **b**, Distribution of extreme-precipitation events relative to the heatwave boundary (days; negative before onset, positive after the end), by decade. **c**, After-versus-before ratio by sub-region and decade.

Heatwaves that occur after drought conditions last longer across all regions (Fig. 5a; Extended Data Fig. S5d). On average, these events are extended by 0.3 days (4.88 versus 4.57 days, +7%), with the most pronounced prolongations occurring in Southern Africa (+0.9 days, +20%) and the smallest in Western Africa (+0.1 days, +3%), and the extension increases with drought severity (+5% for SPEI-3 < −0.5 to +12% for SPEI-3 < −2). The effect on temperature is more variable (Fig. 5b): on average, heatwaves after drought are 1.2 °C hotter (peak Tmax 33.6 versus 32.4 °C); in Southern Africa, they are substantially hotter (+2.7 °C), whereas other regions exhibit more modest increases (+1.5 °C in Western, +0.9 °C in Eastern and +0.3 °C in Central Africa). Because SPEI depends on temperature through evapotranspiration, part of this difference may reflect the persistence of warm conditions from the preceding month.

The proportion of heatwaves preceded by drought varied between decades, from 29.1% in 1985–1994 and 23.3% in 1995–2004 to 32.4% in 2015–2024 (Fig. 5c). Southern Africa shows the steepest pathway, with drought-preconditioned heatwaves reaching 57.5% in the most recent decade. Part of these values reflects where and when heatwaves occur and the background increase in drought frequency, which is itself higher than nominal after 2014 (19.5% of all months with SPEI-3 < −1; Extended Data Fig. S7). Compared with the drought frequency expected for the same cluster, calendar month and decade (23.5%), heatwaves were preceded by drought 1.26 times more often (Extended Data Fig. S8; Extended Data Table S6). This heatwave-specific excess was also found with the precipitation-only SPI-3 (19.3% observed versus 15.9% expected, ×1.21). It reflects a genuine rainfall deficit before heatwaves in Southern (SPI-3 ×1.42) and Eastern Africa (×1.33), whereas in Western and Central Africa it mainly reflects evaporative demand (SPEI-3 ×1.18, SPI-3 ×1.07–1.11). Using the onset month rather than the preceding month raised the share of drought-preconditioned heatwaves to about 38% (Extended Data Fig. S5e), consistent with the heatwave itself increasing evaporative demand in its own month.

**Fig. 5 | Drought amplification of heatwave duration and intensity.** **a**, Heatwave duration and **b**, peak intensity for heatwaves preceded versus not preceded by drought (SPEI-3 < −1 in the month before onset). **c**, Percentage of heatwaves preceded by drought by region and decade.

Classification of PSUs by their dominant compound event combinations reveals that most locations across sub-Saharan Africa are exposed to at most one prominent hazard type (Fig. 6). Using the upper quartile of each metric as threshold, 44.0% of the 53,370 classifiable locations had no prominent event type (Fig. 6a). Single-hazard regimes were the most common otherwise: heat–precipitation events alone (15.2%), post-heatwave precipitation excess alone (14.6%) and drought-heat events alone (13.9%). The combination of drought-heat coupling and post-heatwave precipitation excess represents 9.0% of locations, while the pairing of simultaneous heat–precipitation events with drought-heat coupling (1.8%) or with post-heatwave precipitation excess (1.2%), and the overlap of all three types (0.2%), constitute the least frequent categories.

These shares reflect how the event types relate to each other. Drought-heat coupling and post-heatwave precipitation excess were prominent together 1.47 times more often than expected if they were independent, consistent with heatwaves occurring at the end of the dry season, just before the rains. Conversely, simultaneous heat–precipitation events were rarely prominent together with post-heatwave precipitation excess (0.29 times the expected frequency) or with drought-heat coupling (0.43 times).

These regimes exhibit strong geographical variation (Fig. 6b): heat–precipitation regimes dominate in humid regions (Madagascar, the Gulf of Guinea coast, the Great Lakes), whereas drought-heat regimes and their combination with post-heatwave precipitation excess dominate in Southern Africa and the western Sahel, reflecting dry baseline conditions. **[Check against Fig. 6b: description of Eastern Africa.]** Over time, regimes dominated by heat–precipitation co-occurrence expanded (from 3.6% of classifiable locations in 1985–1994 to 30.0% in 2015–2024), whereas post-heatwave precipitation excess alone became less frequent (26.0% to 7.7%) (Fig. 6c). The same shifts were found among the 18,564 locations classifiable in both decades; at the level of individual locations, however, the regime was unchanged in only 18.3% of cases (14.5% expected by chance), so these changes describe a net redistribution rather than stable local trajectories (Fig. 6f). Results were unchanged when the upper tercile was used as threshold (Extended Data Fig. S9).

Categories denote local co-prominence of independently defined metrics and are descriptive; they do not imply physical coupling between event types.

All main results were reproduced with a single annual heatwave threshold instead of a calendar-day threshold (Extended Data Figs. S10–S13). In that case heatwaves concentrate in the hot season, which raises the after-to-before ratio (3.11) but also its seasonal expectation (1.84), and the deficit of extreme precipitation before heatwaves remains nearly identical (0.63 of the null median).

## Discussion

This study provides, to the best of our knowledge, the first comprehensive assessment of multiple compound-climate-hazard typologies at continental scale for sub-Saharan Africa, drawing on four decades of data at 54,615 georeferenced DHS sampling locations across 34 countries. Three key findings emerge. First, compound climate extremes are becoming more frequent and span all analyzed climate regimes, though their frequency and typologies are strongly shaped by background moisture conditions; same-day heat–precipitation extremes nonetheless remain about three times rarer than expected by chance. Second, extreme precipitation occurs more often after heatwaves than before them, a pattern that exceeds what the rainfall seasons produce, is consistent across sub-Saharan Africa and stable over time, and arises mainly from unusually dry conditions before heatwaves. Third, heatwaves are more often preceded by drought than local drought frequencies would imply, and such heatwaves last slightly longer and are hotter; the share of heatwaves beginning under drought has increased, mostly because evaporative demand has increased.

These findings extend empirical hazard patterns that have previously been documented predominantly in higher-income regions. The increase in drought-associated heatwaves echoes findings from Europe, Asia and North America, where concurrent hot and dry extremes have intensified under warming1,7,8. The underlying physical mechanism is rooted in land–atmosphere feedbacks: soil moisture depletion suppresses latent heat flux, thereby shifting the surface energy balance toward sensible heating and prolonging atmospheric warmth5. Our results are consistent with this process operating across tropical and subtropical African climates: heatwaves were preceded by a precipitation deficit more often than locally expected, most clearly in Southern and Eastern Africa, and heatwaves following drought lasted longer and were hotter. The share of heatwaves preceded by drought reached 32.4% in the last decade, and 57.5% in Southern Africa. However, this rise largely tracks the background increase in SPEI drought driven by evaporative demand, and is not found with the precipitation-only SPI; the heatwave-specific excess itself (×1.26) does not demonstrate a strengthening of land–atmosphere coupling over time.

The asymmetry of extreme precipitation flanking heatwaves has received far less scientific attention in previous work. Our results show that it exceeds what the timing of heatwaves relative to the rainy season would produce, and that it arises mainly from a deficit of extreme precipitation in the weeks before heatwaves rather than from an excess afterwards: compared with the same season in other years, post-heatwave extreme precipitation is not enhanced (0.90 of the expected value). The Clausius–Clapeyron relationship, by which a warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour (~7% per kelvin), therefore cannot by itself explain the pattern. The asymmetry is more consistent with heatwaves developing during anomalously dry spells, through the soil-moisture feedback discussed above and the large-scale subsidence that suppresses convection, with rainfall resuming after the heatwave, including rainfall that terminates it. Because this pattern persists across four decades, exceeds a seasonally constrained null in every sub-region and season, and manifests in 33 of the 34 studied nations, it points to a fundamental atmospheric link rather than a statistical artifact.

The regional differences observed in these compound events are important for climate-risk assessment. Hazard combinations vary across sub-Saharan Africa: same-day heat–precipitation extremes concentrate in the humid tropics, whereas sequences of drought, heat and subsequent rainfall characterise the subtropics of Southern Africa and the Sahel, where drought-heat coupling and post-heatwave precipitation excess are prominent together more often than expected. This diversity indicates that climate risks cannot be fully understood by considering hazards in isolation.

Risk assessments calibrated on European or North American compound event typologies may overlook combinations that are more relevant in other settings like in Africa. Using DHS sampling locations as the spatial grid helps bridge this gap and ensures that the patterns documented here are directly relevant to research addressing societal impacts. These exact spatial coordinates match the clusters where child health, nutritional status and survival metrics are actively monitored21,22.

Several limitations should be considered. While ERA5 reanalysis temperatures provide uninterrupted spatial and temporal coverage, they may underestimate local extremes in regions with sparse observational networks. Similarly, while the CHIRPS precipitation product performs well across Africa, its reliance on infrared satellite estimates can occasionally miss warm-rain events, and the two products use different daily conventions, so that a one-day offset cannot be excluded. Our primary heatwave definition (calendar-day 90th percentile, three consecutive days) targets persistent heat stress relative to the time of year but may not align with definitions used in temperate-climate studies; results with a single annual threshold, closer to recent compound-event work in tropical settings2,23, are reported for comparison. Percentile thresholds were estimated on 1985–2014 and applied without in-base correction, which slightly understates heatwave frequency within that period relative to 2015–2024. The 3-month window of the SPEI-3 drought index integrates precipitation and evaporative demand, but may overlook abrupt flash droughts that can also precondition heatwaves; its temperature-based (Hargreaves) evapotranspiration and its calibration on 1985–2014 make the post-2014 increase in drought frequency partly a reflection of warming, which is why precipitation-only SPI-3 and local drought frequencies were used as comparisons. Clusters sharing the same ERA5 grid cell share identical temperature series; uncertainty estimates and the null model account for this. Finally, the random spatial displacement inherent to DHS coordinates (up to 5 km rural, 2 km urban) introduces minor geographical uncertainty, though this remains negligible relative to the 0.25° grid resolution of ERA524.

Overall, our results demonstrate that compound climate events in sub-Saharan Africa are increasing in frequency and in spatial extent, with a net shift towards regimes dominated by heat–precipitation co-occurrence, while sequences of drought, heat and subsequent rainfall remain a persistent feature of the subtropics. These dynamics carry direct implications for early-warning systems, as these systems remain largely based on single-hazard thresholds, they are ill-equipped to anticipate the combined interactions highlighted in this study. Integrating compound-hazard indicators into existing frameworks therefore represents an urgent and feasible adaptation priority. More broadly, this work provides an empirical foundation for a new generation of climate and risk assessments that moves beyond single-exposure paradigms to quantify the joint and sequential effects of heat, extreme precipitation and drought on child survival, nutrition and infectious disease, precisely the populations and issues where compound risks are likely to have the greatest impact.
